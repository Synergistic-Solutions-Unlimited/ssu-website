The through-line

Nobody asked for any of it.

Every engagement arrives with a scope. The useful work is usually just outside it — visible only if you are close enough to the actual problem to notice, and willing to say so when noticing is inconvenient. That habit, not a sector, is what carries across.

  • Wireless · portfolio strategy

    The brief: get off the most expensive lease sites and find cheaper colocations.

    Several jurisdictions turned out to have no zoning requirement at all — which meant the cheapest long-term answer was not a cheaper lease. It was for the carrier to own the asset outright.

    Taken back to the carrier, who agreed. Became one of the program’s most successful vendors at moving sites off third-party colocations onto carrier-owned towers. A leasing exercise turned into a capital strategy.

  • Wireless · documentation

    The brief: complete the feasibility and site documentation.

    The forms themselves were the problem. Built better ones, for personal workflow, with no intention of them going anywhere.

    Two national carriers independently adopted them as enterprise standard, one across its entire national vendor base. Reviewing other vendors’ submittals against those forms then reached different feasibility conclusions and recovered sites their reviews had ruled out.

  • Wireless · the hinge into software

    The brief: return feasibility and permitting recommendations on nearly 400 relocation candidates, in 60 days.

    It was not achievable by hand, so the answer was not to work harder inside the constraint. It was to change what tool existed. Asked for three days and learned to code from nothing.

    317 feasibility packages across 316 sites, delivered on schedule. Nobody asked for an automation practice. It exists because a deadline made it necessary, and it has been running ever since.

  • Outside work entirely

    There was no brief. A family needed one private place to keep the people who cared about them informed.

    Nothing available fit without asking them to publish their lives on somebody else’s platform. So it got built instead: invitation-only, approval-gated, accessible, private by construction.

    Live in two calendar days, on roughly six hours of hands-on time, and in daily use by the people it was built for. The clearest evidence that the method is not a work persona.

What we build

Systems the people doing the work can actually run.

Operating systems, not tools

A tool solves one task and leaves the operation to hold the rest together. A system carries the whole month: what is true, who decides, what happens on an exception, and what evidence exists afterwards.

Seven of them are in daily production use at a property operator — expenses, property data, inventory, inspections, turnover — reached through one authenticated portal, and run by a five-person team with no technical specialist among them.

Controls that hold when something goes wrong

Versioned packages, schema versioning, staged QA gates that block submission rather than warn about it, deployment preflight, and a rollback path prepared before the change goes in.

Tested by the case that matters. A field submission returned an error after it had already committed. The transaction was identified by its submission ID and payload hash, confirmed, and recovered — instead of a user being told to try again and a duplicate entering a record people trust.

The whole stack, or the part you are missing

From the operating model — sources, authority, states, exception paths — through to shipped software. Engagements start where the work is stuck, not at a preferred product.

That range is demonstrated, not asserted: governed spreadsheets and Apps Script at one end, two signed and installed native macOS applications at the other, and the release discipline that connects them.

Our approach

Context first. Tools second.

Complex operations rarely fail because a team lacks another tool. They fail when source information is scattered, responsibilities are unclear, field activity is hard to trace, and each handoff loses context. SSU organizes those elements into usable operating systems—then applies automation and AI where they make the work more reliable.

SSU does not begin with a preferred software product. It begins with the people doing the work, the assets and records involved, the current source for each fact, the decisions and exceptions that matter, and the evidence needed to know the system worked. Tools are then selected or built around that operating model—and important actions remain visible, reviewable, and accountable to the people responsible for the outcome.
